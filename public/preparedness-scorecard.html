How Prepared Are You Really? A 100-Point Emergency Preparedness Scorecard You Can Complete Today

Published August 11, 2026 · PrepVault

Checklists tell you what to own. They rarely tell you whether you're actually ready — or what single gap is most likely to hurt you first.

This is a different exercise. It's a 100-point readiness scorecard you can complete in about twenty minutes with nothing but your own shelves, a pen, and a piece of paper. It scores you across six categories — water, food, power, heat, communications, and coverage — and it flags the specific red lines where a household becomes genuinely fragile.

The numbers below are a self-scoring standard. They follow common emergency-management planning figures — the 1 gallon of water per person per day, 2,000 calories per person per day, and 72-hour power window that US agencies such as FEMA and the American Red Cross use as baseline guidance in their build-a-kit and preparedness materials. Where your own situation needs more, scale it.

How the scorecard works

Six categories, each worth points. Score yourself honestly — count only what you could actually use today, within the next year, without going shopping. Then add up your total.

1. Water (25 points)

One gallon per person per day — for drinking, cooking, and basic hygiene. Most agencies recommend a 14-day reserve — that's your full-marks target. The math: multiply the number of people in your household by 14.

Target stored gallons = people × 14

Water-purification options stretch that stored supply. Filters and purification tablets turn local water — a creek, a lake, a well — into usable water. Have at least two independent purification options, because a single filter that fails, clogs, or runs out of cartridge is a single point of failure.

Score yourself: 25 points if you can reach 14 days today; score proportionally (e.g., a 7-day supply earns about half).

Red line: fewer than two independent purification options is a genuine fragility — your water plan depends on one thing.

2. Food (25 points)

The goal is a 30-day calorie reserve — not "a lot of canned food," but enough calories to actually run a household for a month.

Count your calories, not your cans. Total up the calories you have on hand, then divide by your daily need. A common planning figure is 2,000 calories per person per day.

Reserve days = total calories ÷ (people × 2,000)

So a two-person household with 120,000 calories on the shelf has a 30-day reserve (120,000 ÷ 4,000 = 30). Open dry goods and rotate what you use. Foods that provide calories you'll actually eat beat a pantry full of items no one touches.

Score yourself: 25 points for a 30-day reserve; fewer days score less.

Red line: a reserve under 7 days is critical. Below that, you're living hand-to-mouth on whatever the grid and grocery store provide.

3. Power (15 points)

Set a target of 72 hours of power autonomy — enough to run heat or cooling, refrigeration, communication, and medical needs through the first days of an outage without the grid.

Three independent ways to make power are stronger than one. Stored fuel for a generator, a solar setup, and batteries each cover a different weakness:

  • Solar works in daylight and needs no fuel, but it depends on the weather. Plan around roughly 5 usable sun-hours per day and accept that a cloudy week cuts that in half.
  • A generator is steady fuel-in, power-out, but it needs fuel stored and rotated.
  • Batteries keep critical devices up at night and in the dark.

Score yourself: 15 points for ~72 hours of combined autonomy; partial coverage scores less.

Red line: under 24 hours of power autonomy leaves you unable to ride out even an ordinary overnight outage, and if you rely on solar, having fewer than 10 batteries makes it weather-dependent — a genuine fragility.

4. Heat (15 points)

If you live in a climate where a grid failure means a cold house, heating isn't optional — it's life safety.

Target 30 days of heating fuel, independent of the grid. One cord of firewood — 4 feet by 4 feet by 8 feet, properly seasoned — commonly runs a stove for roughly 30 days. Propane and other fuels are measured by tank and BTU, not by cord; the point is to hold enough working fuel to close out a cold stretch.

Score yourself: 15 points for 30 days of heating fuel; fewer days score less.

Red line: in a cold climate, relying on a single heat source means one failed appliance, one empty tank, or one blocked flue ends your heat. Two heat sources, or one very robust one with spare fuel, is the difference between inconvenience and emergency.

5. Communications (10 points)

When the power is out, so is the cell network you depend on. Redundancy is the whole game here.

The standard is 3 independent channels, so that no single failure silences you:

  1. A basic cell phone — for day-to-day and for calls you can make in the first hours.
  2. A battery or crank radio — for broadcast alerts when cellular is down.
  3. A two-way radio (HAM/GMRS) or satellite communicator — for coordinating over distances.

Score yourself: 10 points for three channels; fewer channels score less.

Red line: a single communication channel means you're one dead battery away from no news and no way to call for help. No backup at all is the most fragile state on the whole scorecard.

6. Coverage (10 points)

The first five categories cover the survival basics. Coverage is the honesty check: is each core category actually stocked, or have you over-invested in one at the expense of the others?

The classic failure is the household with a fanatical food room but no working water purification, or three radios and no first-aid supplies. Count the core categories you've genuinely stocked — water, food, power, heat, communications, medical, first aid, clothing, lighting, and supplies — and give yourself credit only for the ones you could actually draw on.

Score yourself: 10 points if every core category is stocked; fewer covered categories score less.

Red line: an empty category is the quiet gap. It rarely shows up on a checklist of what you own; it shows up when the emergency is specifically about that category — and it's the one you don't have.

What your score means

Add up your six category scores:

80–100: you have a real, layered reserve. Your work is maintenance — rotation, checking expiry dates, and keeping redundancy from slipping.
50–79: you're on your way, with clear gaps. Your highest-value move is closing your lowest-scoring category, not buying more of what you already over-stocked.
Below 50: you are not as ready as a loaded shelf suggests. Do not buy more canned goods yet. Find your worst red line first — usually water or heat — and fix that before anything else.

Wherever you land, the scorecard is doing its real job: turning "I should be more prepared" into a specific next purchase and a specific date to finish it.

Sharpen the numbers for your climate

Everything above is a temperate-climate baseline. Your real needs scale with where you live:

  • Cold climates burn meaningfully more heat and fuel — figure heating to run somewhere around double to 2.5× for an extreme-cold season, and plan your 30-day heat target accordingly.
  • Hot, dry climates will want water above the per-person baseline — plan roughly 1.5–2× the stored water, and add shade and heat-safety to your plan.

Use the common agency guidance — such as FEMA's build-a-kit guidance and the Red Cross preparedness materials — as your floor and scale up for your conditions. These multipliers are a planning rule of thumb, not a local standard — if you live somewhere severe, let local emergency managers and your own experience set the number for you.

The one question most guides never ask

Checklists ask "Do you have a generator?" This scorecard asks something more useful: "Across all six categories, what is your one weakest link?"

That question is why the format exists. It converts a vague, anxious intention into a number you can act on this week — and into a short, honest brag you can share: "I scored a 74. Here's what I'm fixing." A measured, specific score is what turns good intentions into an actual next step.
